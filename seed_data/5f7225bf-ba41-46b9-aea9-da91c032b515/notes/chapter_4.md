# Can It Happen Here? Authoritarianism and Modern Challenges


## Sinclair Lewis and the Question of Authoritarianism

The lecturer frames the discussion around the 1935 novel *It Can't Happen Here* by Sinclair Lewis. In the novel, Lewis depicts a fascist movement that seizes control of the United States by 1939. While the novel specifically addresses fascism, the lecturer argues that the central question—'Can it happen here?'—is applicable to a broader range of threats within a pluralistic democracy. These threats include creeping authoritarianism, ideological aggression from all sides of the political spectrum, and various forms of political instability. The novel serves as a starting point to complicate the assumption that democratic institutions are immune to collapse or radical transformation. By examining this text, the course explores how democratic societies might experience internal erosion or unexpected political shifts that challenge their foundational values and structures.


## Modern Forms of Bondage

The lecturer highlights that the concept of 'it'—referring to the collapse of democratic norms—can also manifest as the revival of slavery. Despite being a subject of intense debate and research, it is noted that there are likely more people in some form of bondage today than there were during the era of the American Civil War, when accounting for the United States, Brazil, and Cuba. This modern enslavement persists in various forms, including human trafficking, sex trafficking, and labor trafficking, often embedded in the production of global goods, minerals, and resources. These individuals exist in conditions of 'no exit' dead ends, ranging from chattel slavery in tea fields to forced labor in the shrimp fishing industry within the South China Sea. This serves as a stark reminder that historical patterns of exploitation have not disappeared but have instead evolved into contemporary global crises.


## The Shock of Events and Political Instability

The lecturer introduces the concept of the 'shock of events' to describe historical occurrences that are unforeseen and force a reevaluation of societal positions. Examples include 9/11, the attack on Pearl Harbor, the start of the Civil War at Fort Sumter, the 2016 election, and the COVID-19 pandemic. While social history focuses on long-term forces and ideologies, these punctuating events disrupt the expected trajectory of society. The discussion extends to contemporary concerns such as voter suppression, which has evolved from historical disenfranchisement based on race or gender to modern, varied tactics. Furthermore, the lecturer addresses the current public discourse regarding the possibility of a new American civil war. Referencing a recent popular film, the lecturer notes that such a conflict would likely bear no resemblance to the first Civil War, instead presenting a dystopian, collapsed state where the underlying causes of the fighting are often unclear.
