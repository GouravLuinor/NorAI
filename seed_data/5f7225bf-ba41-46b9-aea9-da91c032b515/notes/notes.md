# Introduction to the Course and the Yale Slavery Project


## Course Origins and the Yale Slavery Research Project

This course, part of the Devane lecture series, is fundamentally rooted in the Yale Slavery Research Project. The lecturer, who led this project and authored the assigned book, explains that the course examines how Yale University has been connected to, entangled with, and shaped by slavery and racial ideology from its very inception. The research project was initiated by former Yale president Peter Salovey, who owned the project and requested the lecturer's leadership. The resulting book covers over 300 years of history, concluding in 1915. This specific end date was chosen partly due to a rigid deadline set by the president's office, which coincided with the president's departure from his role. The project involved extensive research, including work by teaching assistants like Emily Yankowitz, who focused on the 18th and 19th centuries. The lecturer notes that this research transformed his understanding of the university, allowing him to recognize the history behind the buildings he walks past daily.


## Historical Scope: 17th Century Indian Wars

The research project and the assigned book begin their historical analysis in the 17th century. A central focus of this early period is the two major Indian wars fought in Connecticut: the Pequot War and King Philip's War. These conflicts are essential to understanding the university's early relationship with racial ideology and its historical context. The lecturer expresses a desire for this initial chapter to have been longer, highlighting the difficulty of containing over 300 years of institutional history within a single volume. The course also concludes its narrative in 1915, specifically examining the story of the Yale War Memorial, a site that many students pass by regularly without knowing its history. This periodization reflects both the constraints of the research project and the specific historical narratives the team sought to uncover regarding Yale's development and its deep-seated connections to the broader American experience.


## The Broader Context of University Historical Inquiry

The lecturer situates this course within a larger trend of universities examining their historical ties to slavery. Over the past two decades, approximately 90 colleges and universities, particularly in the South, have begun investigating their own pasts in relation to slavery. This movement gained significant momentum with reports from institutions like Brown University in 2004-2005 and Georgetown University in 2014-2016, which revealed stories of plantations and the ownership of enslaved people. The lecturer's own center, the Gilder Lehrman Center for the Study of Slavery and Abolition, has been engaged in this type of research for four years, predating the formal birth of the Yale Slavery Project. This course is designed to explore why these institutions are studying their pasts now and what these investigations reveal about the historical imagination and current societal divisions in America.

# Why Universities Study Their Past


## The Legacies of Slavery Project

The lecturer introduces a project titled 'Legacies of Slavery,' which involves collaboration with faculty members from small colleges across the United States. This initiative focuses on investigating the historical past of these institutions, specifically regarding their connections to the problem of slavery. The project is reaching a significant milestone, with a final conference scheduled to take place in Memphis, Tennessee, in the following month. This work reflects a broader trend where numerous small colleges are actively engaging in research to uncover and understand their institutional histories. By examining these historical ties, these universities are participating in a collective effort to confront the complexities of their origins and the enduring impact of slavery on their institutional identities. This ongoing work highlights the importance of historical inquiry within higher education as a means of addressing long-standing questions about institutional accountability and the legacy of slavery in American academic life.


## Curriculum Design and Modern Dilemmas

The course structure is described as a truncated version of the lecturer's broader Civil War and Reconstruction lecture course. The content has been intentionally condensed to focus on the middle portion of the material, which the lecturer argues is essential for understanding contemporary American dilemmas. These dilemmas include issues such as political polarization, societal division, existential threats to the polity, elections that evoke both fear and inspiration, and the general dysfunction of political institutions. By narrowing the scope of the course, the lecturer aims to provide students with the necessary historical context to interpret these modern challenges. The selection of this specific material is driven by the belief that the historical roots of these problems are vital for any meaningful analysis of the current state of American society, politics, and the ongoing struggles that define the national experience today.


## History as Human Tragedy

The lecturer addresses potential student concerns regarding the tone of the course, specifically the risk of being perceived as a 'downer.' He clarifies that while the subject matter deals with difficult topics, it is not intended to be purely pessimistic. Instead, he posits that history has, at its core, a certain kind of human tragedy that is an inherent part of the human experience. By engaging with this tragedy, students are not merely studying distant events but are confronting a reality that they are all part of. This perspective frames the study of history as a reflective process that acknowledges the weight of the past while maintaining a focus on the essential human elements that shape our collective narrative. The lecturer encourages students to remain engaged with the material, emphasizing that understanding this tragic dimension is a necessary component of historical study.

# The Role of History and Human Tragedy


## The Human Tendency to Avoid Historical Tragedy

The lecturer introduces the concept of the human condition, noting that people naturally attempt to circumvent, avoid, or overlook historical tragedy. Drawing on James Baldwin, the lecturer highlights a critical American tendency: we prefer to 'cover up the sleeper' rather than wake them up. This metaphor suggests a desire for comfort and rest, avoiding the 'nightmare' of difficult historical truths. Americans often seek out histories that are fun, hopeful, and focused on victory, rather than engaging with the more challenging aspects of our past. This avoidance is framed as a common human impulse to prioritize comfort over the potentially unsettling reality of historical consciousness.


## Defining a Sense of History

The lecturer recounts an interview between James Baldwin and the oral historian Studs Terkel. When Terkel challenged Baldwin on his assertion that Americans lack a sense of history, Baldwin reflected and provided a simple, profound definition: having a sense of history means knowing that you are not alone. Baldwin explained that whatever one is experiencing—even if it is something horrible happening to an individual, their people, or their country—it has happened before, somewhere, somehow. This perspective serves as a vital grounding mechanism, connecting present struggles to the broader human experience and demonstrating that our current challenges are not unique in the grand scope of history.


## The Utility of Historical Parallels

While the lecturer acknowledges the common saying that 'history repeats itself' or 'rhymes,' they clarify that historical parallels are never entirely identical. Despite this, thinking in terms of parallels is a fundamental way we process information. History is not merely a collection of facts; it is a tool used to ground and root us in stories. These stories can provide inspiration or even moments of joy, but their primary utility lies in their ability to provide context for the present. By recognizing that our current circumstances have precedents, we gain a sense of perspective that is essential for navigating the complexities of the human condition.

# Can It Happen Here? Authoritarianism and Modern Challenges


## Sinclair Lewis and the Question of Authoritarianism

The lecturer frames the discussion around the 1935 novel *It Can't Happen Here* by Sinclair Lewis. In the novel, Lewis depicts a fascist movement that seizes control of the United States by 1939. While the novel specifically addresses fascism, the lecturer argues that the central question—'Can it happen here?'—is applicable to a broader range of threats within a pluralistic democracy. These threats include creeping authoritarianism, ideological aggression from all sides of the political spectrum, and various forms of political instability. The novel serves as a starting point to complicate the assumption that democratic institutions are immune to collapse or radical transformation. By examining this text, the course explores how democratic societies might experience internal erosion or unexpected political shifts that challenge their foundational values and structures.


## Modern Forms of Bondage

The lecturer highlights that the concept of 'it'—referring to the collapse of democratic norms—can also manifest as the revival of slavery. Despite being a subject of intense debate and research, it is noted that there are likely more people in some form of bondage today than there were during the era of the American Civil War, when accounting for the United States, Brazil, and Cuba. This modern enslavement persists in various forms, including human trafficking, sex trafficking, and labor trafficking, often embedded in the production of global goods, minerals, and resources. These individuals exist in conditions of 'no exit' dead ends, ranging from chattel slavery in tea fields to forced labor in the shrimp fishing industry within the South China Sea. This serves as a stark reminder that historical patterns of exploitation have not disappeared but have instead evolved into contemporary global crises.


## The Shock of Events and Political Instability

The lecturer introduces the concept of the 'shock of events' to describe historical occurrences that are unforeseen and force a reevaluation of societal positions. Examples include 9/11, the attack on Pearl Harbor, the start of the Civil War at Fort Sumter, the 2016 election, and the COVID-19 pandemic. While social history focuses on long-term forces and ideologies, these punctuating events disrupt the expected trajectory of society. The discussion extends to contemporary concerns such as voter suppression, which has evolved from historical disenfranchisement based on race or gender to modern, varied tactics. Furthermore, the lecturer addresses the current public discourse regarding the possibility of a new American civil war. Referencing a recent popular film, the lecturer notes that such a conflict would likely bear no resemblance to the first Civil War, instead presenting a dystopian, collapsed state where the underlying causes of the fighting are often unclear.

# Yale's Founders and the Reinterpretation of History


## Institutional Memory and Suppression

Universities are increasingly tasked with probing, unearthing, and telling their own histories. For decades and centuries, institutions like Yale often chose to ignore or suppress uncomfortable aspects of their past. Rather than directly confronting these historical realities, the university historically processed its identity through a 19th-century lens of 'moderate' caution. This approach allowed the institution to walk around its history rather than engage with it. The lecturer emphasizes that this course aims to explore why universities are now changing this approach and why they are finally choosing to confront these buried narratives.


## The Founders and Slavery

Yale's origins are tied to ten clerical founders whose names are prominently displayed on Woodbridge Hall, a building opened in 1901 for the university's bicentennial. The historical reality of these founders includes significant involvement in slavery:

* Seven of the ten clerical founders were slaveholders.
* Woodbridge Hall is named after one of these founders, who was the largest slaveholder among the group.
* This specific individual enslaved approximately 26 people during his lifetime, including Africans, African Americans, and some Native Americans.


## Reinterpretation vs. Removal

The lecturer argues against the removal of historical names from landmarks, suggesting instead that they should be reinterpreted. Woodbridge Hall is described as a glorious national landmark and a beautiful piece of architecture. Rather than removing the names, the lecturer proposes transforming the building into a Yale Museum. This museum would serve as the starting point for campus tours, featuring an exhibition on Yale and slavery at its heart. By contextualizing the names and the history of the building, the university can acknowledge its past while continuing to celebrate its other historical contributions, such as advancements in science and theology.

# The Civil War as a Hinge Point in History


## The Civil War and Reconstruction as Transformative Events

The American Civil War and Reconstruction serve as the most divisive and transformative hinge point in American history. The United States was fundamentally altered by the war, and while Reconstruction was eventually destroyed, its impact remained significant. A central focus of this historical period involves the victories of the war, specifically emancipation and the 13th, 14th, and 15th Amendments. These constitutional changes are not settled history; they remain active sites of conflict. The 14th Amendment, in particular, is a constant source of legal and political struggle, with approximately two-thirds of all litigation in American courts involving it. This ongoing relevance is evidenced by contemporary legal battles, such as those regarding the rights of transgender children to basic education, which rely on Section 1 of the 14th Amendment.


## History vs. Memory

A critical inquiry in this course is the distinction between history and memory. Students are encouraged to consider how they developed personal, family, or collective memories before encountering academic history, which often challenges or complicates those initial understandings. The course aims to explore how history and memory interact, where they diverge, and how they fit together. This process involves examining how individuals reconcile their upbringing with the critical study of the past. By interrogating these concepts, the course seeks to provide students with the tools to analyze the past not as a static set of facts, but as a dynamic field of interpretation that shapes our current understanding of the world.


## The Relationship Between Past and Present

The course emphasizes the inextricable connection between the past and the present. This relationship is exemplified by the work of Marc Bloch, a historian who wrote his influential book, *The Craft of History*, while participating in the French Resistance against the Nazis. Bloch, who was eventually executed by a Nazi firing squad, produced a manuscript that serves as a profound meditation on the nature of historical inquiry and the necessity of thinking in historical ways. His work demonstrates that the study of history is not merely an academic exercise but a vital engagement with the world. By studying the past, we gain insight into the present, acknowledging that our current circumstances are deeply rooted in the historical events that preceded them.

# The Founders, Virtue, and Constitutional Design


## The Intellectual Foundations of the Founders

The American founders were deeply engaged with classical and enlightenment moral philosophy. Jeffrey Rosen, in his book *Pursuit of Happiness*, explores how these figures, despite their own profound personal contradictions—such as Thomas Jefferson owning 200 people while studying moral philosophers—were heavily influenced by thinkers like Cicero and Aristotle. These ancient and enlightenment writers focused on concepts of virtue and the civic good. The founders were not monolithic; they were complex individuals who read widely to inform their political thought. This intellectual background was critical to their approach in crafting the Constitution, as they sought to reconcile their philosophical ideals with the practical realities of governing a new nation. Understanding this context is essential to grasping why they were so preoccupied with the nature of human passion and the potential for factionalism within a democratic society.


## Constitutional Design and Human Nature

Hamilton and Madison were obsessed with the problem of human nature, specifically how to manage the passions and factions that inevitably lead to conflict. They recognized that human beings often fight over both petty and significant issues. To address this, they designed a constitutional structure intended to contain these impulses. Key components of this design included checks and balances, an independent judiciary, and a powerful executive branch. The goal was to create a system that could withstand the inherent volatility of human behavior. As Madison articulated in *Federalist* number 57, the primary aim of a constitution is twofold: first, to secure rulers who possess the wisdom to discern and the virtue to pursue the common good; and second, to implement effective precautions to ensure those leaders remain virtuous while holding public trust. The founders were essentially trying to build a framework that could sustain democracy despite the flaws of the people within it.


## The Question of Democratic Capability

In *Federalist* number one, Alexander Hamilton posed a fundamental question that remains relevant today: whether societies are capable of establishing good government through reflection and choice, or if they are destined to rely on accident and force. This inquiry highlights the founders' uncertainty about whether human beings could successfully create and maintain a democracy. Over 240 years later, the U.S. Constitution has been subject to constant change and intense conflict, most notably during the Civil War, which resulted in three major amendments that fundamentally altered the document. The lecturer suggests that while the founders established a structure to manage human passion, the ongoing struggle to maintain this system continues. The history of the Constitution is a history of being fought over, reflecting the persistent challenge of living within the democratic framework the founders envisioned.

# Why the Civil War Era Still Has a Hold on Us


## Demographics and Personal Connection

The Civil War and Reconstruction era maintains a profound hold on the American imagination, partly due to enduring demographic connections. Despite the nation's increasing diversity and significant immigration from around the world, approximately one out of every three Americans can still trace their family lineage directly to someone who lived through the Civil War. While this does not mean every individual actively researches their ancestry daily, the potential for a personal connection remains high. The lecturer illustrates this with an anecdote about a Korean-born student who, despite having no family history in the United States during the 1860s, became deeply fascinated by the Civil War. When asked why he studied it, the student simply replied that it seemed important. This highlights that the era's significance transcends mere biological heritage, resonating as a foundational period that Americans recognize as inherently important to the national story.


## The Scale of War Loss

A second major reason for the era's lasting impact is the unprecedented scale of loss. Between 1861 and 1865, Americans experienced a level of death in war that remains unmatched. Approximately 750,000 Americans died from military causes, including both combat and disease. This figure does not even account for emerging estimates of civilian deaths, which could range from 50,000 to 100,000, potentially pushing the total death toll toward one million. To contextualize this, the lecturer notes that if the Civil War's per-capita death rate were applied to the Vietnam era, the Vietnam Memorial would require space for nearly 7 million names rather than the roughly 60,000 it currently holds. Such a memorial would stretch across the entire Washington Mall. This immense, collective trauma solidified the era's place in the national consciousness, influencing cultural figures like Walt Whitman, who became a profound poet of death and democracy.


## Epic Narratives and Modernity

Americans are drawn to epic stories—large cultural narratives that explain the nature of a people through themes of suffering, survival, and national identity. These epics often feature clear beginnings, middles, and ends, along with heroes and villains, and can easily become melodramatic. The Civil War fits this mold, especially given the American tendency, noted by William Dean Howells, to embrace tragedy as long as it can be framed with a happy ending. Furthermore, the era marks the onset of American modernity. Modernity here is defined not just by industrialization or skyscrapers, but by the struggles between capital and labor and the creation of 'big government.' The United States underwent a transformation during and after the war that made the nation more modern, a process that involved both growth and the emergence of a 'modern monster'—the massive, efficient Union war machine that ultimately secured victory at Appomattox.


## Moral Convulsion and the Desire for Unity

The Civil War was a 'moral convulsion' that forced society to reorder, reorient, and recreate its institutions through intense, bloody conflict. It was a period where the nation was compelled to destroy itself to be reborn. This leads to a complex relationship with the idea of national unity. Americans often seek to find a positive, unifying narrative out of the horror, racism, and division of the Civil War era. The lecturer points out that institutions, such as Yale, have historically framed the conflict as a struggle where all sides fought for the right as they understood it, ultimately leading to a unified nation. While the desire for unity is understandable and hopeful, it often risks glossing over the deep-seated divisiveness and the brutal reality of the war's origins and consequences.


## The First Great Racial Reckoning

Finally, the Civil War stands as America's first great racial reckoning. This term has become common in recent years, used in the context of events like the 2020 murder of George Floyd or the death of Trayvon Martin. However, the lecturer emphasizes that the nation has engaged in various racial reckonings throughout history, including the election of President Obama and the social upheavals of the 1960s and 1970s. The Civil War remains the primary instance where America destroyed itself and was forced to recreate itself from the graves of 1863, 1864, and 1865. The term 'reckoning' is favored because it suggests that the nation is actively working toward solving its deep-seated racial issues. Whether or not these successive reckonings are successfully resolving the underlying problems, they remain central to the ongoing process of American self-definition.

# Racial Reckoning and the Social Contract


## Defining the Racial Reckoning

The lecturer frames the current American political moment as a 'reckoning.' This concept implies a collective process of gathering and deciding on a path forward. While this might ideally involve active civic participation, the lecturer questions whether modern society is truly engaging in this way, or if it has been reduced to simpler actions like voter registration. The core of this reckoning involves examining whether the United States can maintain a unified social contract. This contract is theoretically grounded in the promise that all people born in the country are citizens and are welcome, as outlined in the three sentences of section one of the 14th Amendment. The fundamental question is whether the nation can live up to these stated ideals in practice, or if the social contract is failing to hold the country together in the face of modern political divisions.


## The Social Contract and Legal Precedents

A central component of the modern American social contract was the Voting Rights Act of 1965, which provided a framework for democratic participation for nearly 50 years. However, the lecturer notes that this foundation was altered by the Supreme Court decision in *Shelby v. Holder*. Beyond voting rights, the social contract is also tested by various cultural issues. The lecturer suggests that court decisions or other shifts can lead to the realization that a unified consensus on these issues may not exist. This instability raises the existential question of whether America can hold itself together when it fails to maintain a unified social contract. The lecturer explicitly references the historical failure of the social contract in 1860-61, which resulted in the dissolution of the Union and the onset of the Civil War, as a cautionary example of what happens when a nation cannot devise a way forward.


## Historical Parallels and Political Division

In discussing modern political division, the lecturer recounts a conversation with a reporter regarding whether America has ever faced an election cycle as divisive or dangerous as the current one. The lecturer emphasizes the importance of avoiding the word 'ever' when describing current political tensions, as this ignores the historical reality of 1860. That period serves as the ultimate example of a breakdown in the social contract that led to Civil War. While some parallels between the 1860s and today do not work, others do. Specifically, the lecturer points to a 'decreasing confidence in institutions that hold us together' as a significant ingredient of division that is present in both eras. Ultimately, the lecturer highlights that when a nation fails to maintain a unified social contract, it must find another way forward to prevent the kind of catastrophic fragmentation seen in the 19th century.
