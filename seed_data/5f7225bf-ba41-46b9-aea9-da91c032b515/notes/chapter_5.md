# Yale's Founders and the Reinterpretation of History


## Institutional Memory and Suppression

Universities are increasingly tasked with probing, unearthing, and telling their own histories. For decades and centuries, institutions like Yale often chose to ignore or suppress uncomfortable aspects of their past. Rather than directly confronting these historical realities, the university historically processed its identity through a 19th-century lens of 'moderate' caution. This approach allowed the institution to walk around its history rather than engage with it. The lecturer emphasizes that this course aims to explore why universities are now changing this approach and why they are finally choosing to confront these buried narratives.


## The Founders and Slavery

Yale's origins are tied to ten clerical founders whose names are prominently displayed on Woodbridge Hall, a building opened in 1901 for the university's bicentennial. The historical reality of these founders includes significant involvement in slavery:

* Seven of the ten clerical founders were slaveholders.
* Woodbridge Hall is named after one of these founders, who was the largest slaveholder among the group.
* This specific individual enslaved approximately 26 people during his lifetime, including Africans, African Americans, and some Native Americans.


## Reinterpretation vs. Removal

The lecturer argues against the removal of historical names from landmarks, suggesting instead that they should be reinterpreted. Woodbridge Hall is described as a glorious national landmark and a beautiful piece of architecture. Rather than removing the names, the lecturer proposes transforming the building into a Yale Museum. This museum would serve as the starting point for campus tours, featuring an exhibition on Yale and slavery at its heart. By contextualizing the names and the history of the building, the university can acknowledge its past while continuing to celebrate its other historical contributions, such as advancements in science and theology.
