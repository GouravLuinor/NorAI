# Introduction to the Course and the Yale Slavery Project


## Course Origins and the Yale Slavery Research Project

This course, part of the Devane lecture series, is fundamentally rooted in the Yale Slavery Research Project. The lecturer, who led this project and authored the assigned book, explains that the course examines how Yale University has been connected to, entangled with, and shaped by slavery and racial ideology from its very inception. The research project was initiated by former Yale president Peter Salovey, who owned the project and requested the lecturer's leadership. The resulting book covers over 300 years of history, concluding in 1915. This specific end date was chosen partly due to a rigid deadline set by the president's office, which coincided with the president's departure from his role. The project involved extensive research, including work by teaching assistants like Emily Yankowitz, who focused on the 18th and 19th centuries. The lecturer notes that this research transformed his understanding of the university, allowing him to recognize the history behind the buildings he walks past daily.


## Historical Scope: 17th Century Indian Wars

The research project and the assigned book begin their historical analysis in the 17th century. A central focus of this early period is the two major Indian wars fought in Connecticut: the Pequot War and King Philip's War. These conflicts are essential to understanding the university's early relationship with racial ideology and its historical context. The lecturer expresses a desire for this initial chapter to have been longer, highlighting the difficulty of containing over 300 years of institutional history within a single volume. The course also concludes its narrative in 1915, specifically examining the story of the Yale War Memorial, a site that many students pass by regularly without knowing its history. This periodization reflects both the constraints of the research project and the specific historical narratives the team sought to uncover regarding Yale's development and its deep-seated connections to the broader American experience.


## The Broader Context of University Historical Inquiry

The lecturer situates this course within a larger trend of universities examining their historical ties to slavery. Over the past two decades, approximately 90 colleges and universities, particularly in the South, have begun investigating their own pasts in relation to slavery. This movement gained significant momentum with reports from institutions like Brown University in 2004-2005 and Georgetown University in 2014-2016, which revealed stories of plantations and the ownership of enslaved people. The lecturer's own center, the Gilder Lehrman Center for the Study of Slavery and Abolition, has been engaged in this type of research for four years, predating the formal birth of the Yale Slavery Project. This course is designed to explore why these institutions are studying their pasts now and what these investigations reveal about the historical imagination and current societal divisions in America.
