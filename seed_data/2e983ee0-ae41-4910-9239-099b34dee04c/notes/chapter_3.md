# Behavioral Finance and the Subprime Crisis


## The Behavioral Finance Revolution

Behavioral finance represents a major revolution in the field that has developed over the last 10 to 20 years. It is defined as the integration of traditional finance theory with insights from other social sciences, specifically psychology, sociology, political science, and anthropology. The lecturer argues that it is a mistake to consider finance in isolation, as there is a vast array of information from these other disciplines that is relevant to financial study. The lecturer has been deeply involved in this field, organizing workshops at the National Bureau for Economic Research since 1991 alongside Dick Thaler from the University of Chicago. This interdisciplinary approach is a central theme of the course and is also explored in the book *Irrational Exuberance*, where the term 'exuberance' is used as a psychological concept to describe market behavior.


## Understanding the Subprime Mortgage Crisis

The subprime mortgage crisis is a significant, ongoing global financial event. 'Subprime' refers to mortgages issued, primarily over the last decade, to subprime borrowers—individuals with poor credit histories or other indicators suggesting a higher risk of default. The subprime lending industry grew dramatically during this period but is now facing severe distress. As the housing market declines and home prices fall, record numbers of borrowers are defaulting on their loans. When a borrower fails to pay their mortgage, the contract stipulates that they lose the house, which then reverts to the mortgage originator. This crisis is particularly noteworthy for its widespread ramifications, exposing fundamental defects in many of the world's largest financial institutions, leading to significant losses, failures, and the firing or resignation of top financial executives.


## Finance as Technology

The lecturer posits that finance should be viewed as a technology—a systematic way of doing things. In this framework, a financial instrument is analogous to an engineering device, possessing a high level of detail and specific functionality. This perspective highlights the technical nature of financial systems and explains why some engineering schools have begun to offer courses in finance. Understanding these financial structures as engineered devices is essential for analyzing how they function and why they occasionally fail, as seen in the systematic nature of the subprime crisis.
