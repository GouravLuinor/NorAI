## Course Philosophy and Scope

Professor Shiller has taught this course for over 20 years and expresses pride in his alumni, many of whom work in finance, though he stresses the course is not strictly vocational. He argues that finance is a fundamental technology underpinning almost all human endeavors, including fields like poetry, where one must eventually navigate financial situations with publishers. The course is intended to be an intellectual exploration of how the world really works, rather than just professional training. Finance is described as a powerful, often hidden force in society. The professor aims to draw out these underlying mechanisms, helping students understand the financial realities that affect everyone, regardless of their eventual career path.


## Financial Markets vs. Financial Theory

Yale offers two primary undergraduate finance courses: Economics 252 (Financial Markets) and Economics 251 (Financial Theory). The distinction was established about eight years ago when Professor Shiller and John Geanakoplos attempted to divide the subject matter. However, they concluded that finance cannot be cleanly split into separate courses on theory and practice, as both are essential to understanding the field. Consequently, the courses are designed to be self-contained; students may take either or both. While there may be some repetition, Economics 251 is more focused on theoretical detail and mathematical rigor, whereas Economics 252 is designed to be accessible to students who are not comfortable with heavy amounts of mathematics.


## Expectations and Course Detail

The professor addresses the level of detail expected in the course. He acknowledges that while some students might find the prospect of extensive detail daunting, it is necessary to understand how the world functions. He draws an analogy to organic chemistry, which is often perceived as having a 'bad reputation' due to its intense detail, yet is fundamentally important for understanding how things work. He clarifies that he does not intend to turn students off, but rather to prepare them for the reality that this course will involve learning many facts. The goal is to connect these facts with financial theory to provide a comprehensive understanding of financial markets.


## Course Materials

The professor concludes the introductory session by identifying the primary textbook for the course. The principal textbook is authored by Frank Fabozzi, with co-authors Modigliani, Jones, and Ferri. This text serves as the foundation for the course's exploration of financial markets and the practical application of financial concepts.

# Course Materials and Resources


## Foundations of Financial Markets and Institutions

The primary textbook for this course is *Foundations of Financial Markets and Institutions* by Fabozzi et al. The lecturer describes this book as very detailed and comprehensive, noting that it covers a vast amount of information. While some students have found it challenging, the lecturer personally found it to be an excellent resource that filled gaps in his own knowledge. Originally, the lecturer required students to master the entire 650-page text; however, he has since adjusted this expectation after hearing feedback from students. Now, the requirement is to focus on the 'key terms' and 'key points' sections provided within the book. These specific areas are considered fair game for examination purposes. The book, co-authored by the late Nobel Prize winner Franco Modigliani, remains a solid and authoritative text on financial markets. Students are expected to use the current edition, though the lecturer may provide specific chapters from newer, unreleased editions on library reserve if necessary.


## Required Reading and Library Resources

The course utilizes a specific reading list that includes both online materials and library reserve items. The lecturer emphasizes that any reading material provided as a clickable link is mandatory. Conversely, books placed on reserve in the library are considered optional background reading. The lecturer explains that he does not expect students to visit the library frequently, as he recognizes the modern preference for online resources. The core philosophy is to ensure students have access to the necessary information without imposing undue logistical burdens. By distinguishing between mandatory clickable content and optional library resources, the lecturer aims to streamline the study process while still providing depth for those interested in further exploration. This approach reflects an understanding of student habits and the evolving nature of academic research in the digital age.


## Additional Textbooks: Siegel and Shiller

In addition to the Fabozzi text, the course assigns *Stocks for the Long Run* by Jeremy Siegel and *Irrational Exuberance* by the lecturer himself. Siegel's book, now in its fourth edition, is described as highly readable and less intense than the Fabozzi text. It focuses on the long-run performance of the stock market and serves as a general treatise on financial markets. The lecturer shares a personal anecdote about his long-standing friendship with Siegel, which began during their graduate studies at MIT. The final required text, *Irrational Exuberance*, addresses the stock market and housing market booms and busts. The lecturer assigns the second edition, published in 2005, which provides context for the housing market peak. These books are available at Labyrinth Books, an independent bookstore in New Haven, which the lecturer supports to help maintain independent operations in the book industry.


## Teaching Assistant Sections

The course structure includes mandatory teaching assistant (TA) sections held during the second half of the week, specifically on Wednesdays, Thursdays, and Fridays. Students are encouraged to review their schedules and select a section that fits their availability before the next lecture. These sections are integral to the course experience, providing a forum for reviewing problem sets and clarifying complex topics. The lecturer expresses high confidence in the teaching assistants and emphasizes the importance of attending these sessions regularly. There are six problem sets throughout the term, which are generally due on Mondays. The TA sections serve as the primary venue for discussing these assignments a few days after submission. Building a relationship with the TAs is highlighted as a key strategy for student success in this large-enrollment course.


## Grading Policy

The grading for the course is based on a structured assessment plan consisting of problem sets, two in-class midterms, and a final exam. The weightings for the final grade are as follows: 10% for the six problem sets, 20% for the first midterm, 30% for the second midterm, and 40% for the final exam. The lecturer notes that while these percentages provide a clear framework, the teaching team will also apply judgment when evaluating student performance. This balance between a rigid grading scale and qualitative assessment is intended to ensure a fair and satisfying experience for all students. The focus remains on demonstrating an understanding of the key terms and concepts covered in the lectures and the assigned readings, rather than rote memorization of every detail in the textbooks.

# Behavioral Finance and the Subprime Crisis


## The Behavioral Finance Revolution

Behavioral finance represents a major revolution in the field that has developed over the last 10 to 20 years. It is defined as the integration of traditional finance theory with insights from other social sciences, specifically psychology, sociology, political science, and anthropology. The lecturer argues that it is a mistake to consider finance in isolation, as there is a vast array of information from these other disciplines that is relevant to financial study. The lecturer has been deeply involved in this field, organizing workshops at the National Bureau for Economic Research since 1991 alongside Dick Thaler from the University of Chicago. This interdisciplinary approach is a central theme of the course and is also explored in the book *Irrational Exuberance*, where the term 'exuberance' is used as a psychological concept to describe market behavior.


## Understanding the Subprime Mortgage Crisis

The subprime mortgage crisis is a significant, ongoing global financial event. 'Subprime' refers to mortgages issued, primarily over the last decade, to subprime borrowers—individuals with poor credit histories or other indicators suggesting a higher risk of default. The subprime lending industry grew dramatically during this period but is now facing severe distress. As the housing market declines and home prices fall, record numbers of borrowers are defaulting on their loans. When a borrower fails to pay their mortgage, the contract stipulates that they lose the house, which then reverts to the mortgage originator. This crisis is particularly noteworthy for its widespread ramifications, exposing fundamental defects in many of the world's largest financial institutions, leading to significant losses, failures, and the firing or resignation of top financial executives.


## Finance as Technology

The lecturer posits that finance should be viewed as a technology—a systematic way of doing things. In this framework, a financial instrument is analogous to an engineering device, possessing a high level of detail and specific functionality. This perspective highlights the technical nature of financial systems and explains why some engineering schools have begun to offer courses in finance. Understanding these financial structures as engineered devices is essential for analyzing how they function and why they occasionally fail, as seen in the systematic nature of the subprime crisis.

# Finance as Technology and Moral Considerations


## Finance as an Evolving Technology

Finance is framed as a powerful, evolving technology, comparable to engines or nuclear reactors. Like these physical machines, financial structures are complex devices consisting of many components that must function correctly. When new financial instruments are first devised, they often encounter difficulties, similar to the early iterations of engines or nuclear reactors. Through the collective experience of many people working over time, a body of knowledge emerges, which constitutes the technology itself. This technology is a potent force in our society, and while it is inherently powerful, it is also dangerous. The subprime crisis serves as an example of the risks associated with the advancement of new financial technology. Despite the problems it may bring, the lecturer argues that we should not allow anger toward the financial community to deflect from the recognition that finance is a vital technology. The goal is to refine and get the technology right, rather than abandoning it.


## Global Adoption and Best Practices

The lecturer notes that while they are not a development economist, they have observed a strong interest in finance when speaking in less developed countries. There is a growing recognition that successful emerging countries are those that have adopted well-developed financial institutions and technologies. These nations must adapt the technology to their specific situations, but they are essentially copying best practices. The lecturer emphasizes that there is nothing inherently negative about copying technology; it is a standard process observed in other fields. For example, when the automobile or the airplane was invented, they eventually became standard across the globe because they represented the best available technology. This universality reinforces the view that this course is fundamentally about understanding finance as a technology that is not unique to any one country, but rather a set of tools that can be implemented globally to foster growth and development.


## Public Perception and Hostility Toward Finance

There is a long-standing conflict and a sense of contempt regarding finance in our society. Some students and members of the public feel that pursuing a career in finance is akin to 'selling out' or valuing money too much. This hostility is partly driven by the fact that many people in the financial community become extremely wealthy. When individuals see that the richest people are often connected to finance, it can trigger jealousy and questions about whether such wealth is deserved. Furthermore, some individuals who achieve significant financial success may act in ways that are perceived as self-important, which does not help their public image. This hostility is not a new phenomenon; historical examples, such as the 18th-century financier William Duer, show that public anger toward wealthy financiers has deep roots. Duer, despite helping finance the Revolutionary War, was chased by an angry mob, partly because his display of wealth, such as liveried servants, was seen as a return to aristocracy.


## The Moral Philosophy of Wealth

The lecturer discusses the moral implications of pursuing financial interests, referencing the 19th-century Yale professor William Graham Sumner. Sumner was a prominent exponent of the idea that individuals should pursue their own interests and that one social class does not owe anything to another. This perspective suggests that there is no need to feel guilty about seeking financial gain, as making money is often a byproduct of doing productive things for the economy, ultimately benefiting society. However, this creates a persistent discomfort. The lecturer introduces the work of philosopher Peter Unger, specifically his book 'Living High and Letting Die', to explore the broader moral dilemma of our economic lives. Most people focus on making money for themselves, but this raises the question of whether such behavior is truly moral when others are suffering from extreme poverty and preventable diseases. This internal conflict is, in the lecturer's view, the same moral dilemma that underlies the financial industry.


## The Moral Dilemma of Personal Choice

Peter Unger's book begins with an address for UNICEF, challenging the reader to consider the impact of their spending. He estimates that for every $3 donated to UNICEF, a life can be saved, as many people die from diseases for which cures exist but are inaccessible due to poverty. Unger asks why the reader does not immediately donate $100 to save lives. The lecturer notes that it is unlikely many readers would actually write that check on the spot. If they do not, they are in some sense responsible for the loss of 30 lives. The lecturer emphasizes that failing to do so does not necessarily make someone a 'bad person', but it does highlight a profound moral dilemma underlying all of our economic lives. This exercise is meant to help students reflect on their own behavior and the choices they make regarding their resources, mirroring the moral complexities faced by those in the financial sector.


## Philanthropy as a Component of Finance

The final point of the discussion is that philanthropy is an integral part of finance. The lecturer expresses a belief that the most successful and important people in the financial industry eventually end up giving their money away. This serves as a concluding thought on how the moral dilemmas of finance can be addressed. By recognizing that financial success can provide the means for significant charitable contributions, the industry can be viewed through a different moral lens. The lecturer encourages students to consider these broader implications as they navigate their own careers and the role of finance in society. The overarching theme remains that while finance is a powerful and sometimes dangerous technology, it is also a tool that, when understood and managed correctly, can be used to achieve productive outcomes and support philanthropic efforts that address global challenges like those highlighted by organizations such as UNICEF.

# Guest Speakers and Course Outline


## The Role of Philanthropy in Finance

The lecturer discusses the limitations of personal consumption, noting that there is a practical limit to how much an individual can consume, such as driving one car at a time. While one could theoretically own many cars, it becomes impractical and inefficient. Consequently, the lecturer argues that for those who achieve significant financial success, the most attractive and appropriate outcome is philanthropy. This perspective on financial markets emphasizes the importance of giving back. To reinforce this, the course will feature guest speakers who exemplify this philanthropic mindset. The lecturer believes that integrating these real-world perspectives is essential for understanding the broader impact of financial markets on society, moving beyond pure theory to examine the motivations and actions of successful practitioners who have contributed to the greater good through their wealth and professional influence.


## David Swenson and the Yale Endowment

David Swenson, a Yale graduate who joined the university in 1985 from Wall Street, is highlighted as a key guest speaker. The Yale endowment is defined as the university's financial assets, excluding items like the art collection or physical buildings, which do not provide liquid income. When Swenson arrived, the endowment was slightly under $1 billion; it has since grown to over $22 billion. His investment strategy has been remarkably successful, yielding a 17% annual return between 1996 and 2006, with specific annual returns of 28% and 22% in recent years. This success has enabled significant campus improvements and construction. The lecturer attributes this performance partly to Swenson's unique academic understanding of investing. Swenson is noted for his rarity in public speaking, making his contribution to the course particularly significant for students.


## Andrew Redleaf and Hedge Funds

The second guest speaker introduced is Andrew Redleaf, another Yale graduate who founded the hedge fund Whitebox Advisors. Redleaf is characterized as an original and creative thinker who maintains an independent perspective, a trait the lecturer considers vital for success in investing. Notably, Redleaf was identified in the media as one of the first individuals to clearly foresee the subprime mortgage crisis, allowing him to profit from the downturn. Despite his success in hedge funds, the lecturer emphasizes that Redleaf also possesses a strong philanthropic side. The inclusion of Redleaf in the course is intended to provide students with insight into how independent, analytical thinking can be applied to identify market trends and crises, while also demonstrating that professional success in finance can coexist with a commitment to social responsibility.


## Course Outline and Risk Management

The course structure differs from standard financial theory courses by focusing on markets and institutions rather than purely mathematical concepts. The lecturer plans to begin with the fundamental theoretical concept of risk management: the universal principle of pooling and hedging risk. The core idea is that while risks cannot be eliminated, they can be spread across many individuals, thereby reducing the impact on any single person. The lecturer uses the example of life insurance, where the death of a parent is a devastating blow to an individual family but a statistically predictable event for society. By pooling these risks, society can provide financial support to affected families, which benefits everyone. This foundational principle of risk management and insurance will serve as the starting point for the upcoming lectures in the semester.

# Risk Management, Insurance, and Portfolio Theory


## Financial Technology and Innovation

Financial technology is evolving and improving continuously, much like engineering or biochemical technology. Over a lifetime, financial institutions will undergo dramatic changes. A key driver of this progress is its fundamental relationship with information technology; computers, the internet, and communication devices make previously impossible financial inventions feasible. Often, an invention may be theoretically sound but economically impractical due to high costs. However, developments in other fields can shift relative prices, suddenly making hypothetical ideas viable. Financial innovation involves experimentation, as abstract theory cannot perfectly predict what will work. Once an invention is proven effective, it is rapidly adopted globally. Historically, financial inventions were not patentable, but this has changed in the United States and other countries, providing a new perspective on financial development.


## Insurance and Probability Theory

Insurance is considered a division of finance that emerged in the 1600s, coinciding with the invention of the mathematical theory of probability. Before this period, the mathematical framework for probability was unknown. Insurance functions by pooling risks, such as life or fire risks, through the issuance of policies to individual policyholders. This historical and theoretical development allowed for the systematic management of risk. The lecturer emphasizes that insurance is fundamentally about managing risk through these pooled structures, which became possible only after the formalization of probability theory.


## Portfolio Theory and Efficient Markets

Portfolio management is a theoretical field that parallels insurance but pools risks differently. Rather than writing policies for individuals, it involves assembling a diversified portfolio or one that is negatively correlated with a specific risk. Key theoretical components include the Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM), the securities market line, and the mutual fund theorem. Beyond these, the Efficient Markets Hypothesis suggests that financial markets function effectively by incorporating information rapidly. This theory, which has roots over a century old, is supported by the observation that market prices adjust instantly to new information. Prediction markets, which forecast outcomes like election results, are often cited as evidence of this 'deep wisdom of the market,' as they frequently outperform traditional polling methods.

# Efficient Markets, Behavioral Finance, and Regulation


## Limitations of Efficient Market Theory

The efficient markets hypothesis suggests that markets are perfectly efficient, implying that it would be impossible to consistently earn excess returns. However, the lecturer argues that this view should not be taken too far. A core lesson of behavioral finance is that markets are not efficient in a global sense because human psychology influences them significantly. The lecturer points to the success of figures like David Swenson as evidence that excess returns are possible for those who look beyond core efficient market theories. Successful investors understand human nature and how it interacts with financial institutions. To better understand these market dynamics, the upcoming material will incorporate research from the psychology department, specifically Kahneman and Tversky's prospect theory, which provides a mathematical and technical framework for understanding how human behavior impacts financial markets.


## The Role of Regulation

Financial markets rely heavily on regulation to function successfully. This includes government oversight, which establishes rules for market participants, and self-regulatory organizations (SROs) created within the industry itself. An example of an SRO is FINRA, formerly known as the National Association of Securities Dealers, which is a membership organization that imposes rules on its members. While these regulations may be perceived as onerous or costly by those in the financial community, the lecturer emphasizes that they are ultimately the 'salvation' of the market. Because not everyone in the financial world is high-minded or acts with integrity, these regulatory structures are essential to make financial markets possible and sustainable.


## Introduction to Debt Markets

Debt is described as the simplest of all financial instruments, consisting of a promise to pay, usually denominated in currency. Debt markets represent a market for time itself, as interest rates are fundamentally tied to units of time. These instruments vary significantly in maturity. In the United States, the shortest-term debt instrument is the federal funds rate, which is an overnight rate with a one-day maturity. On the longer end, the government issues 30-year bonds. The market also includes historical examples of hundred-year bonds and perpetuities, such as the British consols, which have no expiration date and effectively infinite maturity. Understanding these diverse instruments is crucial for analyzing how financial systems manage time and obligations.

# Debt Markets, Stocks, and Real Estate


## Debt Markets and Interest Rates

Interest rates represent the price of time and fluctuate in patterns that serve as vital drivers of the economy. The theory of the term structure explains how interest rates differ based on maturity. While many debt instruments are payable in currency, others are indexed to the price level, providing real interest rates. Historically, real interest rates have experienced significant movements that impact daily life. For instance, the economy recently operated under a regime of negative real interest rates due to aggressive monetary policy by the Federal Reserve. Given the subprime crisis, it is expected that the Fed will likely push real interest rates down again, potentially leading to another period of negative real interest rates.


## Stock Market Mechanics

Stocks represent shares in companies traded on exchanges, but their valuation involves complexities often overlooked. A key ambiguity arises because share repurchases can alter the units of measurement in a security. Management must make critical decisions regarding how much debt to take on—which defines the stock's leverage—and how much in dividends to pay out. These decisions directly influence the stock price. While the concept of dividing a company into shares and selling them is simple, the practical application involves intricate factors. Future discussions will cover the Modigliani-Miller theorem to better understand these financial structures, alongside analyzing the tendency of the stock market to undergo dramatic movements, as observed earlier this year.


## Real Estate and the Housing Market

The housing market is a massive sector, with the total value of single-family homes in the United States estimated at $2 trillion. Recently, this market has become increasingly speculative, leading to unstable home prices. Nationally, real home prices in the U.S. rose by 85% between 1997 and 2006, effectively doubling in value. However, this trend has reversed, with real home prices falling nearly 10% since their 2006 peak. This is not an isolated U.S. phenomenon; many countries worldwide have experienced similar housing booms followed by the beginning of potential busts. Understanding these dynamics requires looking closely at the market for homes and the market for mortgages, which are the primary financial instruments used to fund home ownership.

# Banking, Monetary Policy, and Investment Banking


## Commercial Banking and Regulation

Commercial banks are central features of the economy that have existed for hundreds of years. Their fundamental functions involve operating within the financial system, managing the supply of money, and utilizing the money multiplier. Beyond their basic operations, these institutions are subject to significant bank regulation, such as the Basel Accords (Basel I and Basel II). A critical area of study is the impact of information and technology on the evolution of banking practices. These institutions remain powerful because they facilitate essential economic activities, and understanding their historical role and regulatory environment is necessary to grasp their current influence in society.


## Monetary Policy and Central Banks

Central banks are responsible for managing and stabilizing the economy, primarily by controlling short-term interest rates. Examples of central banks include the Federal Reserve (United States), the Bank of England (United Kingdom), the Bank of Japan (Japan), and the European Central Bank (Europe). These institutions use interest rate adjustments as a primary tool for monetary policy. For instance, in response to the subprime crisis, the Federal Reserve cut interest rates aggressively to support an economy that appeared to be declining. Understanding how these central banks operate provides insight into how they formulate solutions to economic problems and manage national financial health.


## Investment Banking and Financial Intermediaries

Investment banks are distinct from commercial banks because they do not accept deposits from the general public. Instead, they deal primarily with other financial institutions, focusing on the underwriting of securities. This industry is a significant component of the financial community and is subject to its own specific regulations. Beyond investment banking, the financial sector includes professional money managers who oversee portfolios. These individuals operate in highly competitive environments and are often viewed as experts in specific financial technologies. However, their performance is also influenced by behavioral factors, as they are subject to the same interpersonal psychology and human limitations as anyone else. Their success or failure often depends on these human elements as much as their technical expertise.

# Brokerages, Futures, Options, and Democratization


## Brokerages and the Impact of Technology

The landscape of brokerages is undergoing rapid change, primarily driven by advancements in information technology. This transformation is so significant that it is difficult to keep pace with developments. A key feature of this modern environment is the ability to establish an electronic exchange almost overnight, which can then serve as a hub for trading trillions of dollars in securities. This rapid evolution highlights the central role of information technology in the current financial sector. Understanding the dynamics of modern brokerages requires a deep appreciation for how these technological shifts are reshaping the industry. The ability to create massive trading platforms with such speed and efficiency is a hallmark of the current era, fundamentally altering how financial services are delivered and how securities are traded on a global scale.


## Futures and Options Markets

Futures and forward markets are essential components of financial risk management. A forward contract is an agreement between two parties for execution at a future date, typically handled over-the-counter rather than through formal exchanges. In contrast, futures contracts are standardized agreements traded on exchanges. Historically, these were developed in 17th-century Japan for the rice market before spreading globally. Futures markets provide a window into future expectations for various financial variables, such as oil prices, which can be observed years into the future. For example, even when current oil prices reach $100 per barrel, the futures market might be in backwardation, signaling expected price drops. Options markets, which have expanded significantly since the inception of the Chicago Board Options Exchange, provide the right to buy assets. These contracts are vital tools for risk management, and their scope is expected to continue growing rapidly as they become more integrated into financial exchanges.


## The Democratization of Finance

The democratization of finance is a central theme of this course, representing a shift from finance being an esoteric field reserved for a few elite world centers to a system that involves the general population. Electronic technology has made it increasingly economical to offer sophisticated financial services to a broader audience. However, this expansion brings significant challenges, as evidenced by the subprime mortgage crisis. The term 'subprime' refers to bringing individuals into the mortgage market who previously would not have participated. While increasing participation is a core mission, it introduces risks because it involves individuals who may be less knowledgeable about financial concepts and therefore more vulnerable to exploitation. Consequently, while the democratization of finance is a powerful and positive force for inclusion, it requires careful consideration of the dangers and hazards that arise when financial services are extended to those who may lack the necessary understanding to navigate them safely.
