# Introduction to Newtonian Mechanics


## The Foundations of Newtonian Mechanics

Newtonian mechanics serves as the primary framework for understanding the motion of everyday objects, such as trucks, marbles, and billiard balls. This field was established by Isaac Newton and remained the standard for describing natural phenomena until the development of electromagnetism by Maxwell in the 19th century. A central theme of this course is that the fundamental laws of physics are remarkably compact; they can be written on a small portion of a blackboard. The lecturer emphasizes that the goal of the course is to demonstrate how these few core laws can be used to deduce everything else. Students are encouraged to adopt the physicist's approach: prioritize understanding the underlying logic rather than memorizing a vast collection of formulas. As the course progresses, the number of formulas will grow significantly, making rote memorization impractical and prone to failure. By following the logical derivation of concepts, students can avoid the pitfalls of trial and error and gain a deeper, more sustainable mastery of the subject matter.


## Predicting the Future from the Present

The core objective of Newtonian mechanics is to predict the future state of a system given its present state. This process involves selecting a specific part of the universe to study and identifying the necessary information required at an initial time to make accurate predictions. The lecturer illustrates this with a demonstration involving throwing a piece of candy. To predict the path of the candy, one must know its initial location and its initial velocity. Irrelevant details, such as the color of the object or the mood of a person involved in a scenario, are discarded. This focus on essential physical parameters allows for precise predictions. The demonstration of catching the candy serves as a verification of this predictive power; by knowing the initial conditions, one can calculate the parabolic trajectory and position a hand to receive the object at the predicted time and location.


## The Scope and Limits of Physical Modeling

Physics modeling in Newtonian mechanics is characterized by asking highly specific, limited questions about inanimate objects. For instance, when analyzing an object falling from a tall building, the model does not concern itself with the subjective reasons or motivations behind the event, as these are outside the scope of physical inquiry. Instead, the model focuses on quantifiable outcomes, such as the time of impact or the speed at which the object hits the pavement. This deliberate limitation is precisely why physics can boast such high accuracy in its predictions. By isolating the system from extraneous, non-physical variables, physicists can successfully model complex motions. The project of Newtonian mechanics is thus defined as a two-part program: defining the system and its initial conditions, and applying the laws of motion to determine its future state. This approach ensures that the predictions remain robust and verifiable within the defined boundaries of the physical system.

# Kinematics and One-Dimensional Motion


## Kinematics vs. Dynamics

In the study of Newtonian Mechanics, the field is divided into two primary areas: kinematics and dynamics. Kinematics is defined as the complete description of the present state of a system. It provides a list of necessary information to characterize a system at a given moment, such as an object's position and speed, without concern for the underlying causes of its motion. For example, if one throws a piece of chalk, kinematics describes its current location and velocity. Conversely, dynamics addresses the 'why' behind motion—explaining the forces, such as gravity, that cause an object to change its state of motion. While kinematics describes things as they are, dynamics explains how and why they change.


## The Mathematical Point Particle

To analyze motion, we start with the simplest possible model: a mathematical point particle. Unlike a real-world object like a potato, which has spatial extent and orientation (requiring one to track which way its 'nose' is pointing), a mathematical point has no size or internal structure. Rotating such a point does not change its appearance. By treating an object as a point, we avoid the complexities of rigid body rotation. We further simplify the system by restricting the particle's movement to a single dimension along the x-axis. This can be visualized as a bead sliding back and forth on a wire. This one-dimensional model is the most fundamental representation of motion, as it is impossible to reduce the spatial dimensions further.


## Measuring Position and Time

To define the state of our point particle at any given instant, we must establish a coordinate system. This involves: 1. Picking an origin point, defined as zero. 2. Placing markers along the x-axis to measure distance. 3. Using standard units: meters for length and seconds for time. For instance, an object might be located at $x = 5$ meters. It is essential to keep track of these units throughout any calculation; if an answer is provided as a number without units, its physical meaning is lost. While some problems may present data in various units like miles per hour or kilometers per year, one must be able to perform the necessary transformations to maintain consistency. Ultimately, we represent the motion of this particle by plotting its position against time on a graph.

# Velocity, Acceleration, and Calculus


## Interpreting Position-Time Graphs

A position-time graph ($x$ versus $t$) represents the motion of an object. A common misconception is that the shape of the graph mimics the physical path of the object; however, the graph depicts position over time, not a spatial trajectory. If the graph moves up and down, the object is simply moving back and forth along a line, such as crossing the origin and moving to the left before turning around to return to the right. Mathematically, $x$ is a function of time, $x(t)$. While some functions have specific names like $t^2$, $\sin(t)$, or $\log(t)$, a particle's motion can often be described by complex functions that do not have standard names. Understanding how to interpret these graphs is the first step in analyzing motion, as they provide the raw data for calculating velocity and acceleration.


## Average Velocity

Average velocity, denoted by $\bar{v}$, is defined by observing an object at two distinct instances in time, $t_1$ and $t_2$. The average velocity is the change in position divided by the change in time: $\bar{v} = \frac{x(t_2) - x(t_1)}{t_2 - t_1}$. While useful, average velocity does not capture the full detail of an object's motion. For instance, if an object starts at a position, moves around, and returns to the exact same starting position, the average velocity is zero because the total displacement is zero. This result is identical to an object that never moved at all. Because average velocity only considers the endpoints, it is an incomplete description of the motion that occurred between those two times.


## Instantaneous Velocity and Derivatives

To determine velocity at a specific instant, we use the principles of calculus. We consider a small finite time interval $\Delta t$ and the corresponding change in position $\Delta x$. The average velocity over this interval is $\frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t}$, which graphically represents the slope of a straight line connecting two points on the $x(t)$ curve. As we shrink $\Delta t$ toward zero, the straight line becomes tangent to the curve at that specific point. The instantaneous velocity $v(t)$ is defined as the limit of this ratio as $\Delta t$ approaches zero, written as the derivative $v(t) = \frac{dx}{dt}$. This derivative is equivalent to the tangent of the angle $\theta$ of the tangent line on the graph. This process allows us to define velocity 'right now,' similar to how a car's speedometer provides a reading at a single moment.


## Acceleration and Constant Motion

Once the first derivative (velocity) is established, we can take further derivatives. The derivative of velocity with respect to time is called acceleration, $a(t) = \frac{dv}{dt}$, which is also the second derivative of position, $a(t) = \frac{d^2x}{dt^2}$. While one can theoretically take any number of derivatives, only the first two have standard physical names. The third derivative has no common name because no fundamental equations, such as $F=ma$, explicitly require it. We often focus on a specific class of problems where acceleration is constant. A primary example is objects falling near the Earth's surface, which all experience the same constant acceleration $g \approx 9.8 \text{ m/s}^2$. Mastering these constant acceleration problems is a critical skill in mechanics.

# Constant Acceleration and Integration


## The Inverse Problem of Motion

In physics, we often encounter the inverse problem of motion: given a particle's acceleration $A$, we must determine its position $X(t)$. Unlike differentiation, which is an algorithmic process of finding the ratio of changes, integration is a process of guessing. We seek a function whose second derivative is the constant $A$. Knowing that each derivative reduces the power of $T$ by one, we start by guessing a function proportional to $T^2$. If we assume $X(t) = T^2$, the first derivative is $2T$ and the second derivative is $2$. Since we require the second derivative to be $A$, we adjust our guess by multiplying $T^2$ by the constant $A/2$. This yields the function $X(t) = \frac{1}{2}AT^2$, which satisfies the condition that the second derivative is $A$. This represents a particle with constant acceleration $A$ that does not depend on time.


## Generalizing the Position Function

The function $X(t) = \frac{1}{2}AT^2$ is not the most general solution for position. In calculus, when integrating, we can add terms that are 'killed' by the act of differentiation. If we only took one derivative, we could add a constant $C$. However, because we are taking two derivatives, we can add both a constant $C$ and a term linear in time, $BT$. Adding $C$ does not change the second derivative because the derivative of a constant is zero. Adding $BT$ also does not change the second derivative because the first derivative is $B$ and the second derivative is zero. Thus, the most general expression for the position of a particle with constant acceleration $A$ is $X(t) = \frac{1}{2}AT^2 + BT + C$. This logic applies regardless of the coordinate system used, whether we label the position as $X$ or $Y$.


## Physical Significance of Integration Constants

Mathematically, $B$ and $C$ are constants, but as physicists, we must interpret their meaning. Knowing only the acceleration is insufficient to determine the particle's specific history; we need to know its initial state. By evaluating the position function at $T=0$, we find $X(0) = C$. Therefore, $C$ represents the initial position of the object, commonly denoted as $X_0$. To interpret $B$, we take the first derivative of the position function, which gives the velocity: $V(t) = AT + B$. Evaluating this at $T=0$ reveals that $V(0) = B$. Thus, $B$ is the initial velocity of the object, denoted as $V_0$. These constants allow us to distinguish between different objects undergoing the same constant acceleration, such as various objects falling under gravity, by accounting for their unique starting positions and initial speeds.


## The Kinematic Equation for Constant Acceleration

By identifying the constants of integration as the initial conditions, we arrive at the standard kinematic equation for constant acceleration: $X(t) = X_0 + V_0T + \frac{1}{2}AT^2$. This formula describes the position of a particle at any time $T$ given its initial position $X_0$, initial velocity $V_0$, and constant acceleration $A$. For example, in the case of an object falling under gravity, we set $A = -g$ (where $g \approx 9.8$ m/s²), assuming the upward direction is positive. The resulting formula $Y(t) = Y_0 + V_0T - \frac{1}{2}gT^2$ captures the motion of any object falling under gravity. This approach demonstrates how integration allows us to specialize our understanding of motion to a limited class of systems where acceleration is constant, providing a complete description of the particle's trajectory over time.

# Kinematic Equations and Applications


## The Fundamental Kinematic Equation

For any body moving with constant acceleration $a$, its position $x(t)$ at any time $t$ follows a specific quadratic form. This formula accounts for the object's initial state: its starting position $x_0$ and its initial velocity $v_0$. The lecturer explains that knowing these initial conditions allows one to determine the trajectory for all future times. The formula is expressed as $x(t) = x_0 + v_0t + \frac{1}{2}at^2$. The lecturer emphasizes that this structure is intuitive: the $x_0$ term represents the starting location, the $v_0t$ term accounts for motion due to initial velocity (even without acceleration), and the $\frac{1}{2}at^2$ term represents the additional displacement caused by constant acceleration. The factor of one-half arises naturally from the calculus of taking two derivatives to arrive at the acceleration $a$. Understanding this derivation helps avoid rote memorization, as the formula is a direct consequence of the physical definition of constant acceleration.


## Eliminating Time to Find Velocity

To relate final velocity $v$ to initial velocity $v_0$ and displacement $(x - x_0)$ without explicitly referencing time $t$, we can use the velocity equation $v(t) = v_0 + at$. By rearranging this, we can solve for time: $t = \frac{v - v_0}{a}$. This shows that time can be inferred indirectly by observing how much the particle's speed has changed from its initial state given a known acceleration. By substituting this expression for $t$ back into the primary kinematic equation, we eliminate $t$ entirely from the relationship. This algebraic manipulation leads to the celebrated time-independent velocity formula: $v^2 = v_0^2 + 2a(x - x_0)$. This formula is particularly useful when the specific time interval is unknown but the change in position and the velocities are known. The lecturer notes that the variable names (e.g., $x$ vs $y$) are interchangeable as long as the underlying physical constraints remain consistent.


## Summary of Kinematic Relationships

The lecturer highlights two primary equations for constant acceleration motion:

*   **Position Equation:** $x(t) = x_0 + v_0t + \frac{1}{2}at^2$. This describes the location of an object at any time $t$ based on its initial position, initial velocity, and constant acceleration.
*   **Time-Independent Velocity Formula:** $v^2 = v_0^2 + 2a(x - x_0)$. This relates the final velocity squared to the initial velocity squared and the displacement, effectively removing time as a variable.

These equations form a simple paradigm for predicting future behavior based on present knowledge. The lecturer advises against excessive memorization, suggesting instead that students focus on the physical intuition behind these formulas—specifically, how they are derived from the definition of constant acceleration and the initial conditions of the system.

# Solving Physics Problems


## Problem-Solving Strategy and Setup

The goal of this chapter is to demonstrate the application of kinematic equations to predict the motion of an object. The lecturer emphasizes that while memorizing formulas is common, understanding the underlying assumptions is crucial. To illustrate this, a standard problem is introduced: an object is thrown upward from a building that is 15 meters high with an initial velocity of 10 meters per second. The lecturer defines the origin at the foot of the building and chooses upward as the positive direction. For simplicity, the acceleration due to gravity is approximated as $a = -10$ m/s$^2$ instead of the more precise 9.8 m/s$^2$ to allow for mental calculation. By identifying the initial conditions—initial height $y_0 = 15$ m, initial velocity $v_0 = 10$ m/s, and acceleration $a = -10$ m/s$^2$—we can construct the position equation $y(t) = 15 + 10t - 5t^2$. This equation serves as the complete mathematical description of the object's motion under these specific conditions.


## Validity and Assumptions

A critical aspect of applying physics formulas is recognizing the domain of their validity. The derived equation $y(t) = 15 + 10t - 5t^2$ is only valid as long as the object is in free fall under the constant acceleration of gravity. If one were to plug in a time like 10,000 seconds, the formula would yield a large negative position, which is physically nonsensical. This occurs because the assumption that the acceleration is constant at $-10$ m/s$^2$ fails once the object hits the ground. Therefore, one must always remember the terms under which a formula was derived. If an answer seems to contradict physical reality, it is essential to revisit the assumptions made during the derivation to ensure they have not been violated. The formula works until the object impacts the ground, at which point the physical situation changes entirely.


## Determining Velocity

Once the position function $y(t)$ is established, it provides a complete description of the object's location at any given time $t$. To determine how fast the object is moving at any specific moment, we can derive the velocity function from the position function. Since velocity is the rate of change of position with respect to time, we take the derivative of $y(t) = 15 + 10t - 5t^2$. The derivative of this expression with respect to time $t$ gives the velocity function $v(t) = 10 - 10t$. This allows us to calculate the instantaneous velocity of the object at any time $t$ within the valid range of the motion. This process illustrates how kinematic equations are interconnected: once the position is known as a function of time, the velocity can be determined through differentiation, providing a comprehensive view of the object's kinematics.


## Finding the Maximum Height

To find the maximum height the object reaches, we must identify the specific time $t^*$ when the object is neither moving up nor down. At the highest point of its trajectory, the velocity of the object is exactly zero. Using our velocity function $v(t) = 10 - 10t$, we set $v(t^*) = 0$, which gives $10 - 10t^* = 0$, resulting in $t^* = 1$ second. This tells us the object rises for one second before turning around. To find the maximum height, we substitute this time back into our position equation: $y(1) = 15 + 10(1) - 5(1)^2$. Calculating this gives $y(1) = 15 + 10 - 5 = 20$ meters. This demonstrates how to solve for an unknown variable (maximum height) by using a physical condition (velocity is zero at the peak) to determine the necessary time parameter.


## Impact Time and Mathematical Solutions

To determine when the object hits the ground, we set the position $y(t)$ to zero, as the ground is defined at $y = 0$ in our coordinate system. This leads to the equation $0 = 15 + 10t - 5t^2$. Simplifying this by dividing by $-5$ yields the quadratic equation $t^2 - 2t - 3 = 0$. Solving for $t$ using the quadratic formula gives $t = \frac{2 \pm \sqrt{4 - 4(1)(-3)}}{2}$, which simplifies to $t = \frac{2 \pm 4}{2}$. This results in two solutions: $t = 3$ seconds and $t = -1$ second. We discard the negative time solution in this context, as the physical event of the object being thrown occurs at $t = 0$. The positive solution, $t = 3$ seconds, represents the time when the object hits the ground.


## Interpreting Mathematical Results

The appearance of a negative time solution ($t = -1$ s) in the quadratic equation raises the question of why the mathematics provides this result. The mathematical equation $y(t) = 15 + 10t - 5t^2$ is a parabola that does not 'know' about the building or the specific event of throwing a rock. It only knows the initial position, initial velocity, and constant acceleration. The negative solution corresponds to where the parabola would have intersected the ground level ($y=0$) if the motion had been occurring before the clock was set to zero. This highlights that while mathematics is a powerful tool for predicting the future, the results must be interpreted within the context of the physical scenario. Negative time is not inherently wrong, but it must be understood as representing a time before the experiment began, which may or may not be relevant to the specific problem being solved.

# Advanced Mathematical Interpretations


## Interpreting Extra Mathematical Solutions

When solving physics problems, we often encounter mathematical solutions that seem to fall outside our initial expectations. For instance, when analyzing a particle's trajectory, the equations of motion might suggest a position in the past or future that appears physically irrelevant to our current experiment. However, the lecturer emphasizes that we should always listen to the mathematics. An 'extra' solution is not necessarily a mistake; it often contains deep physical meaning. If an equation yields multiple roots, we must consider what each one represents. By blindly discarding solutions that do not match our immediate intuition, we risk missing significant physical phenomena. The mathematics is often smarter than our initial assumptions, and respecting these results is a fundamental practice in physics. When we encounter a solution we did not expect, we must pause to interpret its significance rather than simply ignoring it.


## The Discovery of Antiparticles

A profound example of interpreting extra solutions is found in the work of the physicist Dirac. While studying relativistic quantum mechanics, Dirac sought to determine the energy of a particle, which is related to its momentum $P$ and mass $M$ by the relation $E^2 = P^2 + M^2$. Solving for energy $E$ yields two roots, one positive and one negative. While the temptation is to discard the negative energy solution because energy is typically positive, the mathematics demands that we keep it. Dirac discovered that this negative energy solution was not a mathematical error but a physical reality: it describes antiparticles. This demonstrates that equations can predict new, undiscovered entities. Dirac was not looking for antiparticles, but the theory forced their inclusion. This serves as a powerful reminder that when we formulate laws of motion and solve them, we must accept all resulting answers and seek to understand their physical implications, as they are as valid as the primary solutions.


## Time-Independent Kinematics

In many kinematics problems, we are interested in variables like maximum height or final velocity, but the specific time at which these events occur is not required. If the question of time is not explicitly brought up, we can bypass the process of calculating time by using a time-independent formula. We derive this by noting that at the peak of a trajectory, the velocity $V$ is zero. Using the relation $0^2 = V_0^2 + 2a(Y - Y_0)$, where $a$ is acceleration (often $-g$), we can solve directly for the displacement $Y - Y_0$. For an initial velocity $V_0 = 10$ m/s and $a = -g$, this yields a height of $5$ meters. This approach is highly efficient for finding properties like height or speed at a specific position without needing to solve for the time variable first, simplifying the mathematical labor required for common motion problems.


## Legitimate Differential Manipulation

Calculus is a powerful tool in physics, but students are often warned against manipulating differentials (like canceling $dt$ in $dy/dt$) as if they were simple fractions. However, the lecturer demonstrates that such manipulations are often legitimate in physics when handled correctly. Starting with $dV/dt = a$, we multiply both sides by $V$ to get $V(dV/dt) = aV$. Recognizing that $V(dV/dt) = d/dt(V^2/2)$, we rewrite the equation as $d/dt(V^2/2) = a(dx/dt)$. By treating these as changes over a small time interval $\Delta t$, we can effectively 'cancel' the $dt$ terms. This is valid because the formula represents the limit where $\Delta t$ and $\Delta x$ become vanishingly small. As long as we understand that we are equating changes in quantities over the same infinitesimal time, this manipulation is a standard and powerful technique for deriving kinematic relations without explicit time dependence.


## Integration and Final Results

Building on the manipulation of differentials, we can integrate the changes to find the final kinematic formula. By expressing the change in the quantity $V^2/2$ as equal to $a$ times the change in $x$, we can sum these infinitesimal changes to arrive at the finite result: $V^2/2 - V_0^2/2 = a(x - x_0)$. This simplifies to the familiar kinematic equation $V^2 = V_0^2 + 2a(x - x_0)$. The key takeaway is that we should not hesitate to perform these manipulations, provided we understand the underlying logic: we are looking at how two quantities change in the same time interval $\Delta t$. When $\Delta t$ is made vanishingly small, the relationship becomes exact.
