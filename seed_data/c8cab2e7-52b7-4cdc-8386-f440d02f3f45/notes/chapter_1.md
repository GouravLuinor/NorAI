# Course Overview and Logistics


## Course Content and Philosophy

This course is designed as a year-long introduction to the major pillars of physics. The curriculum spans from classical Newtonian mechanics and the work of Galileo to the significant scientific revolutions of the 20th century, specifically special relativity and quantum mechanics. The lecturer emphasizes that the target audience is broad and diverse, noting that students may have varying academic goals, such as pursuing medicine. The inclusion of topics like quantum mechanics is justified by their foundational importance in understanding the physical world, even if their immediate utility to a student's future career is not yet apparent. The lecturer uses analogies—such as a patient running at the speed of light or a small, restless pediatric patient—to illustrate how these abstract concepts describe real-world phenomena. Ultimately, the goal is to share the fascination that drove the lecturer into physics, ensuring all students are exposed to the most significant intellectual revolutions in the field.


## Administrative Organization and Logistics

The course is being recorded as part of an experimental pilot program funded by the Hewlett Foundation, with the intention of potentially sharing the lectures with a wider audience. Students are instructed to ignore the cameras and proceed as if in a normal classroom setting. The course meets on Mondays and Wednesdays from 11:30 to 12:45. The course website is the primary hub for all administrative communication, including posting problem sets and important announcements. Students must be officially signed up for the course to receive these emails. Homework is assigned on Wednesdays and must be submitted before the following Wednesday's class. The head TA, Mara Barabas, manages the collection and return of problem sets. Solutions are posted on the afternoon the homework is due, which is why late submissions are generally not accepted unless a student presents a highly compelling and verified personal reason, such as a medical emergency or significant family event.


## Grading Policy and the Amnesty Plan

The final course grade is determined by a weighted average: 20% for homework, 30% for a midterm exam in October, and 50% for the final exam. To ensure fairness and provide students with a safety net, the lecturer implements an 'amnesty plan.' Under this policy, the lecturer compares the student's overall grade calculated from the weighted average against the grade earned solely on the final exam. The higher of these two scores is used as the final grade for the course. This plan is intended to provide hope and motivation after the midterm, particularly for students who may be new to physics and need time to 'crack the code' of how to succeed in the subject. The lecturer emphasizes that if a student demonstrates mastery of the material on the final exam in a closed environment, their performance on earlier assignments becomes secondary.


## Support and Discussion Sections

Beyond the main lectures, the course provides multiple avenues for student support. Mara Barabas, as the head TA, is the primary point of contact for administrative issues. Academic support is provided through discussion sections led by faculty members. Postdoctoral fellow Mark Caprio holds a discussion section on Tuesdays from 1:00 to 2:00 PM in the Sloan Lab. Additionally, assistant professor Steve Fullernetto holds a section on Tuesday nights in Dunham Lab, room 220. The Tuesday night timing is specifically chosen to assist students as they work on the homework due the following morning. Regarding office hours, the lecturer has not yet finalized a schedule, noting that the location of his office in the Sloan Lab makes it less convenient for students than meeting before or after class. He plans to solicit feedback from the class via email to determine the most suitable time for office hours.
